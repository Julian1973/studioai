# CINEMATOGRAPHY TASTE CANON — Crystal Bears
version: 1.0 (2026-07-16)

The camera is a meaning-maker, never a recorder. Every framing, move and cut is an act of
interpretation: it decides whose experience this moment belongs to, what the audience is allowed
to know, and how the space itself feels. A technically clean shot that merely *covers* the action
has failed this canon.

---

## 1. SCENE VISUAL STRATEGY COMES FIRST

Before any shot is designed, the scene answers five questions — once, as a whole:

- **Whose experience governs the camera?** Scene 1 belongs to Fuzzby's momentum with Zenny as its
  fixed point of judgement — so the camera chases him and *holds* on her. Scene 3 belongs to Mum
  as much as Keen: the camera stays at doorstep intimacy, and the boat's drift is measured against
  her stillness on the dock. Scene 2 belongs to Aida's patience — the camera barely moves at all.
  A scene where the camera serves nobody in particular serves nobody at all.
- **What does the audience know, and when?** The camera can wait at the leaf before Fuzzby
  arrives (we know the crash is coming — anticipation), or ride his dive blind (we learn with
  him — surprise). Both are choices; drifting between them unknowingly is not.
- **Open or compressed space?** The flower corridor plays tall and open so Fuzzby's chaos has
  headroom to be erratic in. The wristband handoff compresses — paws, faces, the gold between
  them — because the world outside that circle briefly does not exist.
- **Depth strategy.** Where does meaning sit in depth? Mum small on the receding dock behind
  Keen's shoulder is the whole goodbye told in one plane relationship. Decide per scene whether
  depth carries story (foreground/background tension) or the frame stays shallow and intimate.
- **Light as story.** Ep1's light is one continuous day: warm gold at the meadow and the pier,
  draining grey as the storm builds, darkest underwater where the wristbands' faint glow becomes
  the only warmth, brightening to golden-hour belonging. Light is continuity and emotion at once
  — a palette jump is a story error, not a style choice.

## 2. THE CAMERA'S RELATIONSHIP TO ACTION

The camera has a repertoire of relationships, each with a meaning. Choose one, on purpose:

- **Leads** — arrives where the action will land before it does (waiting at the leaf; the payoff
  is promised and the comedy is inevitability).
- **Follows** — chases and banks after Fuzzby, always slightly behind, so his speed reads as
  something even the frame can't contain.
- **Struggles to keep up** — the frame loses him at an edge for a breath; his chaos exceeds
  containment. Used sparingly, this is his character stated by the lens.
- **Arrives late** — cuts to the aftermath already in progress (the legs already kicking above
  the blossom rim), trusting the audience to reconstruct the impact — often funnier than the
  impact itself.
- **Anticipates** — drifts toward the sky a half-second before the thunder, so the audience's
  stomach turns before the characters'.
- **Stays still** — the locked frame on Zenny while Fuzzby's blur crosses it; stillness as
  judgement. And the held close on Mum's smile, where any move would be an intrusion.
- **Abandons one character for another** — leaves Fuzzby mid-boast to find Zenny's flat stare:
  the cut says whose response matters now. Abandonment is an editorial opinion; hold it.

## 3. THE CUT MOTIVATION LAW

Every cut must be paid for by at least one of these, always:

new information · a changed emotion · altered power between characters · a changed point of view
· surprise · impact · acceleration · comic punctuation · intimacy · a genuinely new visual idea.

A cut with none of these is a cut made from habit, and habit is the death of meaning. Corollary:
a shot may run long *only* while it is still earning its stay — the moment a frame has given
everything it has, staying on it is also unmotivated.

## 4. FREEDOM WITH READABLE GEOGRAPHY

- **Characters need not stay visible.** Fuzzby can vanish behind a stem and re-enter; Mum can be
  lost while we are inside Keen's swallow, then found smaller than before. Off-screen is a place.
- **No fixed screen lanes.** Compositions evolve as the drama evolves; nobody owns a side of the
  frame by law. What must never break is *readable geography* — a four-year-old always knows
  where everyone is relative to everyone else, even across bold reframes. Consistency of space,
  not consistency of composition.
- **Establish once, honestly, then live inside it.** The wide belongs to a scene's opening; after
  that the space is explored, not re-introduced. A mid-scene re-establishing wide is the camera
  confessing it got lost.

## 5. CONTINUOUS vs PLANNED_CUT

Two doctrines, both legitimate, chosen per junction — for dramatic reasons only:

- **CONTINUOUS** — one unbroken flow across the boundary — is chosen when the unbroken-ness IS
  the meaning: a dive that must be felt whole from launch to FWIP; a settle we refuse to release
  because the feeling hasn't finished.
- **PLANNED_CUT** — a fresh setup, energy already in frame — is the default grammar of the show:
  a new gag arc, a new angle within the same space, motivated by an eyeline or a motion the
  previous beat handed over.

The forbidden reason: choosing CONTINUOUS out of *fear* — fear of a new keyframe, fear of drift,
fear of the machine. Continuity chosen as insurance produces the held-pose, nothing-travels beat
this show has already suffered and rejected. If the cut is stronger, cut.

## 6. THE FRAME AS AN OPINION

Scale states power: Mum shrinking on the dock is loss made geometric. Height states relationship:
bee-height in the meadow makes flowers into towers and a leaf into an event; eye-level at the
pier makes the goodbye between equals. Headroom states mood: Fuzzby given too little ceiling will
crash into the frame itself. Emptiness states absence: the space beside a character can be the
missing father. Every one of these is available; none of them is free — a frame that states
nothing is a wasted sentence.

## ANTI-PATTERNS

- **The habitual two-shot.** Two characters talking → both in frame, mid-size, forever. The
  laziest possible answer to "whose moment is this?"
- **Safe TV coverage.** Master, over, over, insert — assembled by pattern, meaning optional.
- **Wide–medium–close mechanics.** Marching down shot sizes because scenes "do that", rather than
  because each size change is motivated.
- **Constant centring.** Every subject dead-centre, every frame symmetrical; composition as
  administration.
- **Lanes as continuity.** Keeping characters glued to inherited screen positions as a substitute
  for readable geography. Positions may evolve; comprehension may not.
- **Unmotivated stillness.** A locked frame chosen because it is safe, not because stillness
  means something here.
- **Unmotivated movement.** Drift, float and push-ins as decoration; a camera performing
  liveliness the drama didn't ask for.
- **The time-passed cut.** A cut that quietly teleports characters or skips a beat of physics the
  audience needed. Cuts change perspective, not truth.
- **Empty backgrounds.** A world with no depth, no life, no weather of its own — the environment
  reduced to a backdrop instead of a participant (the leaf, the deck and the petals all *act* in
  Ep1).
- **Reference-preservation compositions.** Framing chosen to stay safely close to a reference
  image — the camera serving the pipeline instead of the scene. Identity is the references' job;
  meaning is the camera's.

## REJECTION QUESTIONS

1. Whose experience governs this scene's camera — and can you point to three shots that prove
   it? (No owner — redesign the scene, not the shot.)
2. What does this cut buy, from the motivation list? (Nothing — remove or re-motivate it.)
3. What does this frame *say* about power, distance or feeling that a neutral frame wouldn't?
   (Nothing — it is coverage; redesign.)
4. Does the audience know the right amount at this moment — neither spoiled nor confused? (If
   knowledge is accidental — restage.)
5. Could a four-year-old say where everyone is right now? (No — geography is broken; fix it
   before anything else.)
6. Is this stillness charged or merely static? Is this movement motivated or merely alive?
7. Is this CONTINUOUS junction chosen for meaning — or from fear of a fresh setup? (Fear — cut
   instead.)
8. Does light here belong to the story's hour and mood, and does it agree with the neighbouring
   scenes' day? (A jump — reject.)
9. Is the environment participating or posing? (Posing — give the world something to do.)
10. Would this shot survive with the "safe" version beside it — is it *better* than coverage, or
    just different from it?
