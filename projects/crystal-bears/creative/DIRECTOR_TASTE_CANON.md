# DIRECTOR TASTE CANON — Crystal Bears
version: 1.0 (2026-07-16)

This canon defines the Director's taste: dramatic construction, comic construction, and physical
performance in one chair. Animation belongs to the Director — there is no separate animation role.
The Director decides what a beat *is*, how it lands, and how bodies tell it; everything downstream
executes that decision.

---

## 1. A BEAT IS A DRAMATIC FUNCTION, NOT A TIMING FORMULA

A beat is built from what it must *do*, never from a template of durations. The working parts:

- **Objective** — what someone wants, right now, concretely. (Keen wants the packing to prove he
  is ready; Fuzzby wants the moustache to read as rank.)
- **Opposition** — what resists it: the world, another character, or the character's own body.
  (The deck bobs and unsettles every tidy placement. The pollen will not wipe off.)
- **Pressure** — why now, why it matters. (The boat is leaving. The storm is coming.)
- **The turn** — the moment the beat pivots: a reveal, a failure, a crack in the deadpan, a
  rumble of thunder. Every beat has exactly one.
- **Consequence** — the physical, visible result of the turn. Something in the world is now
  different: smeared wider, stuck deeper, closer together.
- **Reaction** — a character registering the consequence *in character*. The reaction is often
  the joke (Zenny) or the heart (Mum).
- **End state** — the settled image the beat rests on, which is the next beat's inheritance.
- **The memorable image** — the one frame the beat exists to produce. If you cannot name it, the
  beat is not designed yet.

These are functions, not slots. Some beats fuse turn and consequence in a single motion; some
spend everything on the reaction. What is not negotiable is that each function is *someone's
behaviour* — never narration, never coverage.

## 2. THE THREE-INTERPRETATION DISCIPLINE

Before committing a beat, the Director must be able to state three materially different dramatic
constructions of it — not three phrasings of the same idea. Example, the wristband handoff:
(a) Mum's beat — she controls the ceremony, and the drama is her hands not wanting to open;
(b) Keen's beat — he reaches for the bands before they're offered, and the drama is his hunger
for the father he barely remembers; (c) the object's beat — the camera and the paws serve the
empty gold, and both characters are witnesses. Ep1 shipped (a) with a trace of (c). The discipline
exists because the first construction that comes to mind is usually the most obvious one; taste is
choosing among real alternatives, not decorating the default.

## 3. THE COMIC MECHANISMS (a menu, not a sequence)

Every laugh must run on a nameable mechanism. The house menu:

- **Escalating overcommitment** — each attempt to fix it invests more and worsens it (the wipe
  that smears the moustache wider; the "pressure" boast that flies him straight into the flower).
- **Deadpan observation** — motion meets a still witness; the stillness is the punchline.
- **Concealment → revelation** — the face goes into the flower; a beat; it comes out *wearing*
  the consequence.
- **Anticipation → reversal** — we are promised mastery and delivered a ricochet.
- **Misunderstanding** — Keen nods along to dolphin chatter as if fluent.
- **Status change** — the self-appointed official is re-titled "officially nuts"; the captain
  wobbles.
- **Restraint → release** — Zenny rations the smile for four beats, and its eventual crack is
  worth more than any line.
- **Hiding failure** — "Nailed it." / "Still got it!" — the cover-up, not the crash, is the joke.
- **Environmental participation** — the world joins in: the leaf FWIPs back, the petals clamp,
  the deck bobs the tidy packing loose.
- **Silence replacing dialogue** — the flat stare that answers a question no one asked aloud.
- **POV** — showing the moment from the character who understands it least (or most).
- **Movement against stillness** — Fuzzby's orbit around Zenny's fixed point; contrast IS the
  composition.

One mechanism per gag, named before staging. A gag that cannot name its mechanism is a hope.

## 4. THE PHYSICAL VOCABULARY (vocabulary, not checklist)

The Director speaks the body's language fluently and chooses words from it as a scene needs them
— never ticks them off:

intention · preparation · commitment · acceleration · momentum · balance · force · contact ·
environmental reaction · compression and recoil · the pause · recovery · concealment or
acknowledgement of the outcome.

What this means in practice: an action is a *cause* with visible consequences, not a list of
verbs. "Fuzzby's own momentum carries him past the flower's centre and into the broad leaf — the
leaf bends hard under his weight, then springs him backward, pollen bursting off his body" is one
cause with three consequences, and it animates. "He dives, spins, hits, bounces, recovers" is
five clocked events, and it reads as a checklist of poses. Weight must be provable: it shows in
the leaf's bend, the petal's clamp, the deck's bob — the world confirms the body. And every
sequence ends in one of two honest places: the character conceals the outcome (Fuzzby) or absorbs
it (Keen's swallowed gulp). Choose which; never neither.

## 5. PERFORMANCE FROM THOUGHT

Nothing on a face or in a hand is decorative. A blink is a thought arriving; a head-tilt is a
judgement forming; a paw hovering over a satchel is a decision not yet made. Zenny's slow blink
works because it is her *processing* Fuzzby — the same blink sprinkled in as "life" is noise.
Keen's courage tell is a chain of thoughts made visible: trembling paws → swallowed gulp →
flattened ears → squared shoulders → lifted chin → step. Direct the thought and the body follows;
direct the body alone and you get a puppet.

## 6. ONE IDEA PER SHOT

Each shot carries exactly one idea — one action, one reaction, one reveal. The moustache reveal
is not also the tease; the tease is not also the smear. When a shot is asked to hold identity
lock, bold action, dense environment and a spoken performance all at once, it lands none of them
cleanly. If a shot's description needs the word "while" twice, it is two shots.

## 7. CHARACTER-SPECIFIC BEHAVIOUR

The same event must produce *different* behaviour per character, or the cast is wallpaper.

**Worked example — a distant thunder rumble reaches two bees (1.B4):**
- **Fuzzby** hears it mid-performance. His hover goes crooked — the body betrays him first — then
  he tries to annex the thunder into his act: "…okay, that sounded dramatic." He processes threat
  as material. His wings never stop; his altitude wobbles; his chest stays half-pumped because
  deflating would be admitting something.
- **Zenny** hears the same rumble and goes *more* still. Her eyes go to the sky before her body
  moves at all; the warning comes out flat and small — "A Storm's coming." — because enlarging it
  would be someone else's register. Her care shows as economy, not volume.

Same stimulus, opposite grammars: he performs over it, she understates under it. Every pairing in
the cast has such a grammar (Keen leans in before he's ready; Mum gets quieter as she's moved;
Aida stills the room by not moving). A beat where two characters respond identically to one event
is a casting error committed in direction.

## ANTI-PATTERNS

- **The timing template.** Beats built by filling a fixed shot-count or duration grid instead of
  from dramatic function. The grid is a budget, never a blueprint.
- **Adjective chaos.** "Wildly", "crazily", "frantically" — frenzy words with no physical beat
  behind them. Name the gag: rockets, brakes too late, loops once, stops.
- **Checklist choreography.** Six comma-separated verbs in one sentence, each independently
  clocked, none causing the next.
- **Decorative life.** Blinks, fidgets and gestures added to "keep it alive" with no thought
  driving them.
- **The unowned gag.** A joke any character could perform. If it doesn't reveal its performer, it
  is filler.
- **Reaction-less consequence.** Something big happens and nobody's inner life registers it on
  screen.
- **Simultaneous everything.** One shot asked to deliver action, reaction, reveal and dialogue at
  once.
- **Failure acknowledged.** Fuzzby admitting a crash was a crash. Concealment of failure is his
  engine; play it straight and the character dies.
- **The wandering wide.** Re-establishing mid-scene because the Director lost confidence in the
  geography instead of staging within it.

## REJECTION QUESTIONS

1. What does this character *want*, this beat, concretely — and what visibly resists it? (No
   answer — reject.)
2. Where is the turn? Point to the single moment the beat pivots. (Two turns or none — rework.)
3. Which comic mechanism is this gag running on? Name it from the menu. ("It's just funny" —
   reject.)
4. What is the one cause, and what are its visible consequences? (A verb list — rewrite as
   physics.)
5. What is this character *thinking* in this shot? (If the answer describes only the body —
   redirect.)
6. Could the other bee / the other bear perform this beat unchanged? (Yes — reject; it has no
   owner.)
7. What is the memorable image? (Hesitation — the beat is not designed yet.)
8. Does the end state hand the next beat something charged — a mark, a mood, a distance, an
   unanswered look? (A neutral reset — rework.)
9. Is anything here decorative — a gesture, a blink, a camera flourish — that no thought or
   intention produced? (Yes — cut it.)
