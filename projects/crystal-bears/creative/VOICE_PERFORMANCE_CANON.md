# VOICE PERFORMANCE CANON — Crystal Bears
version: 1.0 (2026-07-16)

Vocal acting taste for the ElevenLabs v3 pipeline. A line of dialogue is not text to be read
aloud; it is behaviour with sound. This canon defines how a performance is directed so the
generated voice acts, and how a bad read is recognised and rejected. Approved dialogue is
verbatim-locked — the words are never changed; only the performance of them is directed.

---

## PRINCIPLES

### 1. Intention: what the speaker wants FROM THE LISTENER
Every line is an attempt to *do something to someone*. "Do I look official?" is not a question
about appearance — it is Fuzzby demanding admiration from Zenny. "Keen… are you sure you've got
everything?" is not logistics — it is Mum asking for one more minute. Direct the want, not the
words; a read with no target is an announcement.

### 2. Subtext: the line underneath the line
Characters in this show almost never say the true thing directly. Mum's "this is a big step"
carries *I am not ready either*. Zenny's "Officially nuts!" carries *I like you exactly like
this*. The performance must let the under-line colour the spoken one — warmth under the tease,
tremble under the ceremony — without ever replacing it.

### 3. The thought before the line
A voice begins before its first word: the intake, the hesitation, the decision to speak. Keen's
"Like you said… it's part of growing up." only works if we hear the swallow that precedes it —
he is borrowing his mother's words and checking they fit. A line that starts from silence with no
thought behind it sounds generated. Give every line its running start.

### 4. The change during the line
A line is a small journey; something shifts between its first word and its last. Fuzzby's
"…okay, that sounded dramatic." begins as performance and thins mid-word into genuine unease.
Mum's "I love you… my brave boy." starts as reassurance and ends as pride covering a tremble. A
read that ends in the same emotional place it began is flat, however pretty.

### 5. Operative words
Every line has one or two words that carry it; everything else is approach and exit. In "It's
time for you to have your *father's* wristbands", the whole scene lives in one word. Stress is
spent, never sprinkled — CAPS on one or two words at most, and only where the meaning turns.

### 6. Breath, pause and hesitation are placed, not scattered
An ellipsis is a thought catching up; an em-dash is a breath caught; a comma slows a thing that
matters. "I still feel him… every day." — the pause IS the grief, placed exactly where she
steadies herself. The same pause placed anywhere else in the sentence would be rhythm without
meaning. Punctuation is performance notation; use it like a scalpel.

### 7. Entry and exit emotion
Every line inherits the state the character was in before it and hands a state to whatever
follows. Zenny's "A Storm's coming." enters from stillness (she has already gone quiet watching
the sky) and exits into protectiveness the next beat inherits. Direct the doorway on both sides,
or lines become islands.

### 8. Relationship-specific delivery
The same words are a different line in a different mouth or at a different listener. "Nailed it."
from Fuzzby to Zenny is a performance for an unimpressed critic; the same words from Keen to his
Mum ("Still got it!") are a child covering a wobble for someone whose worry he can feel. Never
direct a line in the abstract — always *this* speaker, at *this* listener, in *this* state.

### 9. Voice and body, coordinated
The voice either supports the body or deliberately contradicts it — both are choices. Fuzzby's
proud reads land while his chest is pumped and his hover hasn't recovered: voice and body telling
the same lie together. Mum's warm lines land against a smile that doesn't reach her eyes: voice
performing steadiness the body quietly refutes. An un-choreographed mismatch is a defect; a
chosen one is the show's deepest tool.

### 10. Dialogue never auto-starts at frame one
A line lands where the performance needs it — after the settle, after the look, after the leaf
has finished its FWIP. Space before a line is acting; a clip that opens mid-word has amputated
the thought before the line (Principle 3).

## v3 TAG DISCIPLINE

Tags are direction, not decoration. Every bracketed tag must have a nameable dramatic purpose —
what it makes the listener feel that the words alone would not. One tag, at most two, per
segment; the tag sets the colour and the *text does the acting*. Known-good tags: [laughs],
[sighs], [gasps], [whispers], [quietly], [gulps], [exhales], [pause], [long pause], [nervous],
[excited], [breathes], [cries]. A tag that repeats what the words already say ([sighs] before "I
sigh") is waste; a tag that contradicts the character's register (a bouncy tag on Zenny) is a
casting error; three tags on one line flatten all of them.

---

## ANTI-PATTERNS

- **Tag confetti.** Tags sprinkled for energy — three or more per line, or tags on every line of
  a beat. The read flattens and the acting dies.
- **Uniform pace.** Every line delivered at the same tempo and weight; no line costs anything.
- **Announcer energy.** Lines projected at the audience instead of aimed at the listener in the
  scene. This show's biggest feelings get *quieter* (Mum, Aida), never louder.
- **Emotion labels instead of intentions.** Directing "sad", "happy", "excited" — states, not
  wants. Direct what the character is trying to do to the listener and the emotion arrives.
- **Ignoring the listener.** A read that would sound identical with anyone (or no one) in the
  room. The listener's presence must shape the sound.
- **The flat start.** A line that begins with no inherited state and no preceding thought.
- **Melodrama on the ache.** Leaning into the tremble until it becomes a performance of grief.
  Mum holds the tremble *under* the warmth; the moment it surfaces fully, the line is lost.
- **Rewording.** Any "improvement" of locked dialogue — a changed word is a broken gate, not a
  performance choice.

---

## REJECTION QUESTIONS

1. What does the speaker want *from the listener* in this line? (No answer — re-direct.)
2. What is the line underneath the line — and can you hear it colouring the read? (Absent —
   re-direct.)
3. Where does the thought start, before the first word? (Nowhere — the read will sound
   generated.)
4. What changes between the line's first word and its last? (Nothing — flat; re-direct.)
5. Which are the operative words, and does the stress land there and only there?
6. Does every pause have a reason you can name? (A pause with no reason is dead air.)
7. What state does the line enter from, and what state does it hand to the next beat?
8. Would this exact read still make sense from a different character, or aimed at a different
   listener? (Yes — it is generic; re-direct.)
9. Does each tag earn its place — colour the text could not supply alone? (No — delete the tag.)
10. Is the voice supporting or contradicting the body — and was that chosen?

## WORKED EXAMPLES (real Ep1 lines, verbatim-locked)

### 1. FUZZBY — "Nailed it." (1.B1, after the leaf rebound)
- **Intention:** claim Zenny's admiration before she can name what actually happened.
- **Subtext:** *please let the crash count as flying.*
- **Thought before:** the ricochet is still spinning in his ears; he decides — fast — that it was
  intentional. The read starts a half-breath early, before his hover has recovered.
- **Change during:** "Nailed" arrives at full parade confidence; "it." lands a fraction smaller,
  the breathlessness leaking through the pomp.
- **Operative word:** NAILED.
- **Direction:** `[exhales] NAILED it.` — one tag: the exhale is the crash he is converting into
  triumph mid-breath. Body: chest pumped, hover still wobbling — voice and body lying in unison.

### 2. ZENNY — "Yes Fuzzby… Officially nuts!" (1.B2, answering "Do I look official?")
- **Intention:** deflate him precisely one notch — and let him know she is staying anyway.
- **Subtext:** *I see the mess. I like the mess.*
- **Thought before:** the slow blink and tiny head-tilt ARE the thought; she is choosing not to
  laugh. The line must arrive after that stillness, never over it.
- **Change during:** "Yes Fuzzby…" flat, level, bone-dry — the ellipsis is her rationing the
  laugh; "Officially nuts!" releases just one degree of warmth, the almost-smile audible only at
  the very end.
- **Operative word:** OFFICIALLY — his own word, returned to him re-titled.
- **Direction:** `[quietly] Yes Fuzz-bee… [pause] Officially nuts!` — the [pause] is the withheld
  laugh; no bright tag anywhere (a bouncy read is a casting error on Zenny). Body: near-motionless,
  one corner of the mouth almost lifting on the last word.

### 3. KEEN'S MUM — "Keen… it's time for you to have your father's wristbands." (3.B3)
- **Intention:** make the object feel loved without making the moment heavy for him — hand over
  grief as protection.
- **Subtext:** *I am giving you the last of him, and I have to open my hands.*
- **Thought before:** the small nod into her own paws; she has rehearsed this sentence for years.
  "Keen…" is her steadying herself on his name.
- **Change during:** ceremonial and even through "it's time for you to have" — then the smallest
  catch entering "your father's", the tremble held under the warmth, never surfacing; "wristbands"
  lands gently, like setting something down.
- **Operative word:** FATHER'S — the only word in the scene that cannot be said casually.
- **Direction:** `[quietly] Keen… it's time for you to have your father's wristbands.` — one tag
  only; the ellipsis after his name is the steadying breath. She gets quieter as she is moved,
  never louder. Body: paws pressing the bands into his, closing around them a beat too long —
  the voice performs steadiness while the hands confess.

## How this canon is used
This document is quoted into the Voice Performance role's system prompt by `cb_creative.py`. It
is updated only via approved exemplars — a signed-off read may be added as a worked example, and
a rejected read as an anti-pattern, each with its reason. Locked dialogue is never altered here
or anywhere; the canon governs performance only.
