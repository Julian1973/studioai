How to use this guide
This is a directing system for turning an approved script beat, continuity frame, character references and dialogue audio into a cinematic Seedance generation. It is not a catalogue of fashionable camera words. Its job is to make the action readable, the reactions truthful, the cuts motivated and the last frame useful.
THE CENTRAL DOCTRINE  Direct the change, not the movement. A clip becomes cinematic when the audience clearly feels what changed and when the camera is in the right place to reveal that change.

The official Seedance 2.0 material confirms multimodal text, image, video and audio references, prompt-driven camera planning and 15-second multi-shot audio-video output. It also acknowledges remaining weaknesses in detail stability, multi-subject consistency and complex edits. The practical rules in this guide are a production methodology designed around those strengths and limitations; they are not an official ByteDance prompt manual.
The director's north star
One clip, one dramatic change. The clip may contain several actions, but they must serve one turn in the story.
One audience priority at a time. Tell the viewer where to look before asking the model to add more motion.
Reactions complete events. An impact, reveal or line is not finished until its effect is registered.
Every cut has a reason. Cut for information, emotion, consequence, rhythm or concealment—not variety.
Every ending is designed. The final image is an editorial button and the continuity source for what follows.
The ten questions before any prompt

3. Landing beats: how a clip finishes
A landing beat is the moment after the main event when its meaning becomes legible. It is not dead air. It is the last deliberate piece of storytelling: the audience receives the result, reads the reaction and carries a clear image into the next clip.
The five functions of a landing
Confirm. Make the new state undeniable: the flower settles, the object is broken, the character has arrived.
Interpret. Show the reaction that tells us whether the result is funny, frightening, touching or suspicious.
Punctuate. End on a concise comic or emotional button instead of explaining beyond the moment.
Stabilise. Compose a clean, readable final image with no unfinished camera move or ambiguous pose.
Hand off. Preserve position, gaze, prop state, light direction and screen geography for the next generation.

Listener acting
Keep the mouth closed. Only the active speaker lip-syncs; the listener reacts with eyes, breath, posture and timing.
Give the listener an intention. They may be evaluating, resisting, hiding amusement, waiting to interrupt or trying not to worry.
Protect the line. Do not layer a large listener action over the key spoken phrase unless the story requires interruption.
Let the response arrive after the words. A tiny delay often feels more human than an instant model-generated expression change.
Reaction scale for ages 4–8

5. Motivated coverage and cuts
A cinematic clip is not one that contains many cuts. It is one in which every edit changes what the audience knows, feels or anticipates. If the new angle adds no new information, keep the shot.
Seven valid reasons to cut
Reveal. Show information unavailable from the current angle.
Reaction. Transfer ownership from the action to the person affected.
Consequence. Show what the event changed in the world.
Line. Give a speaker or listener the precise coverage needed for performance.
Motion. Cut on an exit, impact, turn, gesture or change of direction.
Concealment. Let an action happen off-screen so the result is funnier, safer or more surprising.
Rhythm. Shorten or prolong attention at a genuine comic, emotional or suspenseful threshold.
CUT TEST  Complete the sentence: “Cut here because the audience now needs to see…” If the sentence has no strong ending, do not cut.

MOVEMENT PRINCIPLE  Track with physical movement. Push for inner change. Pull back for new context. Pan to follow. Whip only for speed. Settle for the landing.

Camera imperfection can add life
Perfect mechanical centring can make animation feel synthetic. A controlled camera may lag a fraction behind Fuzzby's reckless flight, lose him at the frame edge, then recover through a motivated cut. The imperfection must be designed and brief; random focus hunting, arbitrary shake and incomplete reframing read as mistakes.

Rack focus as a story action
A focus pull should transfer narrative ownership from one meaningful subject to another. Specify both ends of the path and the trigger. “Cinematic shallow depth of field” is not enough.
FOCUS-PULL LANGUAGE
Hold Zenny sharp in the foreground while the background remains soft. A flower suddenly compresses behind her. Her eyes react first. As Fuzzby rises into view beyond the flower, rack focus smoothly from Zenny to Fuzzby; do not hunt or pulse between planes.

Using out-of-frame action
Create anticipation. Let the subject leave frame while the witness tracks them with their eyes.
Hide the difficult or unnecessary moment. The collision may happen just outside frame; the consequence can be cleaner and funnier.
Make the environment react. A plant compresses, pollen erupts or a shadow crosses before the subject returns.
Protect geography. The return must come from the established direction and distance; never teleport or re-enter from nowhere.

Comedy requires truthful physics
Squash-and-stretch can exaggerate force, but the audience still needs cause, resistance and recovery. 9. Dialogue, audio and speaking ownership
When approved dialogue audio exists, it is the timing master. Visual direction must fit around the recording. Speaker ownership must be explicit because multi-character scenes remain a known consistency risk.
The audio contract
One asset, one role. Identify the exact audio reference and state whether it supplies dialogue, timing, voice performance or all three.
Map every line. Name the speaker at the moment the line occurs; preserve exact wording and punctuation.
Close inactive mouths. During Fuzzby's line, Zenny listens with her mouth closed; during Zenny's line, Fuzzby remains silent.
Use real timing. Only include timestamps when the actual audio has been measured.
Do not invent sound. No extra words, vocalisations, humming, reactions or narration unless approved.
SPEAKER-OWNERSHIP LANGUAGE
Treat @Audio1 as the absolute master track and only voice source. Use its opening silence for the physical gag; both mouths remain closed until the first spoken word. 14. Failure diagnostics and repair
When a generation fails, change only the smallest instruction responsible for the failure. Preserve the parts that worked. Broad rewrites make it impossible to learn which directing choice improved the result.

Quick reference: director's language
Need
Use language like
Motivated edit
Cut on his exit / cut as the line changes her / cut to reveal the consequence.
Reverse coverage
Reverse medium close-up, preserving the established axis and eyeline.
Focus transfer
Hold A sharp; on B's arrival, rack focus smoothly from A to B.
Off-screen action
He clears frame right; the witness follows with her eyes; the environment reacts before he returns from the same direction.
Reaction timing
Her eyes move first; after one restrained breath, the meaning reaches her face.
Comic restraint
The verdict stays flat and near-still; the smile appears only after she turns away.
Camera lag
The camera pans after him but deliberately fails to keep up; cut on his exit.
Living hold
The camera settles; action resolves; only breath, wing movement and drifting pollen remain alive.
Snap landing
The first corner of the smile appears; cut immediately—do not linger.
Causal impact
Contact compresses the flower; the stem bends; petals rebound; loose pollen trails through the light.
Continuity
End with X screen left, facing right, holding Y at chest height under the same left-side key light.
Failure-specific constraint
Only his face contacts the flower; his body remains outside and visible.
