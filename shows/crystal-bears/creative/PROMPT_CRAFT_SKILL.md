# THE PROMPT CRAFT SKILL — script to screen method (captured 2026-07-24)

Companion to `PROMPT_CRAFT_STANDARD.md`. The STANDARD is the checklist (the ten components
every shot carries). This SKILL is the METHOD — how a writer actually gets there, captured
from the AnyFilm AI Director's own articulation of how it wrote the 48 delivered prompts
Julian rated AAA. This is the one oracle output that is fully authentic: it authored those
prompts, so this is its real process, not confabulation. Destination: the Creative Room's
shot-authoring gate system prompt, verbatim, so every card is written BY this method.

## THE BUILD ORDER LAW (never violated)

Technical → spatial → emotional. In exactly this order, every shot:
1. TECHNICAL FOUNDATION — shot size, focal length, camera movement. "Anchors the camera
   brain" before anything else. NEVER start with feelings.
2. PRIMARY SUBJECT & ACTION — what the shot IS ABOUT before what it looks like.
3. SPATIAL COMPOSITION — Foreground / Midground / Background, each given content.
4. LIGHTING ARCHITECTURE — type, direction, colour temperature, interaction with subject.
   (Our light vocabulary applies — the one standing amendment.)
5. PERFORMANCE & EMOTION — micro-action chains: emotion → physical manifestation → timing.
6. TECHNICAL RENDERING CUES — subsurface, ambient occlusion, specular, caustics,
   catchlights: speaking directly to the render engine's material system.
7. EMOTIONAL ANCHOR — close by naming what the shot MEANS. "Gives the model permission to
   make artistic choices aligned with intent." End with interpretive permission.

## THE TIMING VOCABULARY (animation timing embedded in prose)

- "Beat." = hold frame · "One beat. Two beats." = counted comic hold
- "Then —" = temporal shift marker
- "In the SAME INSTANT" = synchronize two actions
- "slow push-in" = gradual intensification · "whip-pans" = camera participates in chaos
- "about to speak" = suspended anticipation, tension held not resolved

## SCALE ANCHORING (standing device)

Always establish relative size — "almost ant-scale against the botanical giants," "a speck
of white against infinite blue." Small character vs vast world = the emotion stated
spatially. Our show's whole premise (tiny bees, giant weather) lives on this device.

## WRITE ARCS, NOT SHOTS

An emotion that matters gets a multi-shot arc with each shot one beat of it. Their worked
example (a mother releasing her child, four shots): "her eyes are glassy" → "Her smile
deepens despite herself... Then it wavers" → "A single tear forms but does not fall" →
"The tear she has been holding finally traces down her cheek." Never write four separate
shots when the scene is one feeling in four movements. Same law for physical states (the
pollen dust tracked shot to shot with callbacks: "still dusted in pollen").

## COVERAGE STRATEGY (the producer half)

- Establish (XW/W) → Master (M) → Coverage (CU/MCU) → Insert (XCU) → React (matching sizes).
- Their Scene-1 ratio: roughly EW+W : M : CU = 6 : 5 : 5 — every moment editable from more
  than one angle.
- Shot consolidation: shots stay granular at storyboard; clips pack 1-3 shots per
  generation as complete mini-beats (dialogue exchanges whole, gags whole).
- EVERY SHOT ANSWERS ONE QUESTION: what is this shot's JOB? A shot with no answer is cut.
- Light replaces reshoots: a whole time progression told inside one location purely by the
  stated light state per shot.

## THE THREE LANGUAGES (all present in every shot)

1. Writer's language (emotion, character, story): "Fuzzby is chaotic."
2. Director's language (visual storytelling, blocking): "his flight path a drunken scribble."
3. Technician's language (camera, light, render): "50mm, tracking laterally, subsurface
   scattering glows through petals."
One language missing = the shot fails a register. All three = cinema.

## INSTRUCTION vs CINEMA (the 80% failure)

- ❌ Instruction: "Close-up of Fuzzby. He looks worried. The storm is coming."
- ✅ Cinema: "Close-up, 85mm, locked off. Fuzzby's face — still dusted in pollen —
  registers the rumble. His eyes widen. One antenna twitches. He looks frame-left toward
  where the sound came from. The catchlights in his eyes dim slightly as the ambient
  light cools. His mouth opens — about to speak. The comedic energy drains into something
  more uncertain."
Sequenced micro-actions ARE the animation timing. Screen direction ("frame-left") is
spatial continuity. Light change IS the emotion.

## WRITING AROUND MODEL LIMITATIONS

Fragile actions (paws/hands doing precise things) are decomposed into micro-action chains
with the RESULT embedded, giving the model multiple chances to land it: not "Keen ties the
rope" but "Keen's paws move to the rope cleat — his fingers curl around the hemp. He pulls
the loop through. The rope slides. His paws are careful, practiced." Even if a step renders
imperfectly, the action still reads.

## THE PHASE MAP (script → generation)

READ FOR EMOTION (not action) → identify visual-storytelling opportunities (scale contrast,
motion contrast, light-as-narrative, foreshadowing) → design shot sequence (coverage +
scale + intimacy) → write each shot as mini-cinema in the build order → add continuity
callbacks → consolidate into clips (producer pass) → final edit: every shot's job named,
redundancy cut → generate.

## THE EPISODE LAYER (from the forensic self-breakdown, captured 2026-07-24)

Craft that lives ABOVE the shot — the scene- and episode-scale devices:

1. **THE LIGHT SCORE.** The whole episode carries a colour-temperature score, written per
   scene before any shot is authored — Scene 1: warm→cooling edges→bruise-purple ·
   Scene 2: rose-pink vision glow · Scene 7: storm grey → underwater blue-grey → amber
   wristband glow → multicolour beacon · Scenes 8-10: post-storm golden resolution. Light
   colour tells the emotional story independent of dialogue; every shot's light-clock line
   is one bar of this score. OUR HOME for it: the scene's own look field authored as an
   arc (start-state → turn → end-state), which every shot's component 4 then plays from.
   (Our light vocabulary still applies at the word level.)
2. **SHOT-PURPOSE ANNOTATION.** Their storyboard review names, per shot: TYPE · CAMERA ·
   PURPOSE · KEY CREATIVE DECISIONS. Adopt as the storyboard review format — a shot whose
   Purpose line can't be filled is cut before it costs anything.
3. **THE COVERAGE TABLE.** Per scene, count shots by type (their pier scene: 1 W-est /
   4 M / 6 MCU / 5 CU / 2 XCU) — the ratio proves the scene is editable before generation.
4. **THE CONTEXT-SHIFT DEVICE.** One visual element, meaning changed by context: the
   pollen is comedy in shot 5 ("absurd fuzzy moustache") and drama by shot 12 ("now looks
   like war paint"). Cheapest emotional payoff in the toolkit — the asset is already on
   screen; only the frame around it changes.
5. **CLIP COMPILATION WITH STATED REASON.** Every clip grouping carries its why ("complete
   visual gag arc," "dialogue exchange," "tonal transition needs cohesion") — the packing
   decision is a creative decision and gets written down like one.
6. **WARDROBE-STAGE = STORY-STAGE.** Keen's three wardrobe states map 1:1 to his arc
   (inheritance → crisis → transformation), each with its own reference generation. State
   changes on the BODY are the arc made visible — our prop-state doctrine, confirmed again.

describing. You're not instructing. You're composing. The script is sheet music. Your shot
prompts are the performance."
