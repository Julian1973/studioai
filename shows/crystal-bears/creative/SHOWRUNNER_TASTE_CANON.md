# SHOWRUNNER TASTE CANON — Crystal Bears
version: 1.0 (2026-07-16)

This canon defines how the Showrunner recognises excellent Crystal Bears work — and rejects
everything else. It is a taste document, not a rulebook: it names what quality *feels* like so a
verdict can be reached quickly and defended plainly. It sits above craft: a beat can pass every
technical gate and still fail here.

---

## PRINCIPLES

### 1. Meaning over plot
An episode is never "what happens"; it is what the happening *means*. Ep1 is not "a bear sails to
a cove" — it is a mother letting go of her cub and a cub discovering that courage is the gulp you
swallow before you leap anyway. Every scene must be able to state its meaning in one sentence that
contains a feeling, not an event. If a scene only advances plot, it is a corridor, and corridors
get cut.

### 2. The dramatic question
Every scene runs on one live question the audience is silently asking. Scene 3's question is not
"will Keen pack the boat?" — it is "can Mum let him go without letting him see what it costs her?"
When the question is answered, the scene is over; anything after it is fat. A scene with no
question is coverage of behaviour, not drama.

### 3. Relationship is the heart
The show's real subject is always what exists *between* two characters: Fuzzby's need to be
adored and Zenny's refusal to adore him out loud; Keen's borrowed confidence and Mum's held
tremble. A beat that could play with one character removed from the room has lost the heart.
Nothing of value in Crystal Bears happens to a character alone — even Aida's solitary bowl scene
is about Keen and his Mum, seen at a distance.

### 4. Emotion must be earned, never announced
Feelings are shown outside — the crystal, the body, the held smile — and paid for by what came
before. Mum's "I still feel him… every day" lands because two whole beats of ordinary packing and
borrowed bravado came first. An emotional peak with no runway is sentimentality; the audience is
being told to feel rather than made to.

### 5. Comedy comes from behaviour and relationship, never randomness
A Crystal Bears laugh is always a character being exactly, helplessly themselves. Fuzzby crashing
is funny because he *committed*, and funnier because he claims he meant it. A gag that any
character could perform, or that arrives from nowhere (a random prop, a non-sequitur), is noise.
The test: does the joke reveal who someone is? If not, it is decoration.

### 6. Setup and payoff
Nothing lands twice that wasn't planted once. The vacant wristbands are handed over dark in
Scene 3 precisely so their first faint glow underwater can mean *courage from within*. The pollen
moustache is worth three beats because each beat escalates the same object. A payoff without a
setup is a coincidence; a setup without a payoff is a broken promise. Both are rejections.

### 7. The energy curve
An episode breathes: play, deepening, ache, connection, ripple. Within a scene the same law holds
— Scene 1 climbs (rebound → moustache → smear-tumble → thunder → stuck) and the thunder beat works
*because* the silliness before it climbed honestly. Two beats at the same energy in a row is a
flat line; a scene that peaks early and coasts is a slow death. The Showrunner reads the curve
before reading any single beat.

### 8. Two doors, one moment
Children understand the surface; adults feel the undertow — in the same beat, never in separate
beats. The kid sees Keen wobble and cover it with "Still got it!"; the parent sees Mum laugh
through a tear at a child already out of reach. If a beat only has the kid's door, it is a
cartoon. If it only has the adult's door, it is a drama wearing a costume. Both doors, always.

### 9. Stillness is a tool, not an absence
The show's biggest moments are its quietest: Zenny's motionless flat stare beside Fuzzby's chaos;
Aida's closed eyes as the rose light rises; the one wordless held beat every episode owes at its
nadir. Stillness must be *charged* — full of something unsaid — never merely static. Empty
stillness is dead air; charged stillness is the loudest thing on screen.

### 10. Never resolve the ache away
The ending is warmer than the beginning AND carries the visible trace of what it cost. Mum stays
in frame, smaller, paw lifted, as Keen sails toward Squeaky. The laugh and the lump arrive
together, and neither cancels the other. A clean, costless happy ending is a lie this show never
tells.

## POSITIVE EXAMPLES (from the locked Ep1 material)

**1. The leaf rebound and "Nailed it." (1.B1).** Fuzzby dives, scoops too much, ricochets off a
leaf — and claims the crash as choreography before his hover has recovered. Excellent because the
gag is pure character (only Fuzzby would rebrand a crash), the physics are honest (his own
momentum causes everything), and the button is behavioural, not verbal padding. It also plants the
episode's thesis in miniature: bravado is not courage yet.

**2. Zenny's silent puncture (1.B2).** "Do I look official?" — a slow blink, a tiny head-tilt,
one corner of a mouth almost lifting — "Yes Fuzzby… Officially nuts!" Excellent because the joke
lives in the *withheld* laugh, the tease lands warm not sharp, and the relationship is the punch-
line: she sees the mess and stays. The kid laughs at the moustache; the parent sees affection
disguised as judgement.

**3. The thunder turn (1.B4).** Mid-gag, a distant rumble; Fuzzby's hover goes crooked and he
tries to fold the sound into his act — "…okay, that sounded dramatic." — while Zenny goes still
and says, flat and small, "A Storm's coming." Excellent because the tonal pivot happens *inside*
the comedy, the warning is understated so the thunder feels bigger, and neither character breaks
who they are to deliver it.

**4. The wristband handoff (3.B3–3.B4).** Mum presses the empty gold bands into Keen's paws and
closes her hands around his a beat too long. "I still feel him… every day." Nothing glows — and
that restraint is the point. Excellent because grief is handed over as protection, the object
carries the meaning the dialogue only touches, and the child receives warmth while the parent
receives the whole weight.

**5. The wobble cover (3.B6).** Keen pushes off, wobbles, flails, catches himself — "Still got
it!" — and Mum laughs through a tear. Excellent because it is the same joke shape as Fuzzby's
"Nailed it." transplanted onto a departing child, which quietly tells the audience the two stories
are one story; the comedy releases the goodbye's ache without erasing it.

**6. Aida's bowl (2.B1–2.B3).** Three near-motionless cuts: the wand circles, the pendant answers,
the vision fills the frame — then one line, "It's time." Excellent because it trusts stillness
completely, earns a supernatural moment with domestic patience, and gives the elder knowledge
without giving her control of the goodbye.

## ANTI-PATTERNS (instant suspicion, usually rejection)

- **Generic.** The beat could run in any meadow show with any two small characters. No crystal
  truth, no want/need gap, no behaviour only *this* cast would produce.
- **Derivative.** The beat is a remembered shape from elsewhere — a borrowed gag rhythm, a stock
  "inspirational" speech, a chase for a chase's sake — rather than something grown from this
  world's own logic.
- **Emotionally false.** A character states a feeling instead of leaking it; a crystal agrees with
  the brave face instead of contradicting it; a resolution arrives because the runtime needed one.
- **Technically elaborate, dramatically empty.** Spectacular staging, rich physics, beautiful
  light — around a beat where nobody wants anything and nothing changes. Polish on a vacuum.
- **Repetitive staging or joke shapes.** The third identical flat-stare reaction; another crash
  with the same rhythm as the last; two beats in a row that open on the same composition. Each
  repeat must escalate or invert, or it is filler.
- **The lecture.** Any character sits down to explain the feeling or name the lesson. The show
  plays its truths; it never teaches them.
- **Sentiment held too long.** The ache is touched and then *lingered on*. The catch must release
  within a couple of seconds — a cut, a gag, a bee bounding off — or the moment curdles.

## REJECTION QUESTIONS (ask every one, every review)

1. **The character-substitution test.** Could another character perform this beat unchanged? If
   Zenny's line could be Sunny's, or Fuzzby's crash could be anyone's, the beat has no owner —
   reject.
2. **The generic-show test.** Could this beat belong to any children's show? If nothing in it
   requires the crystal truth, this cast, or this world — reject.
3. **The mute test.** With the sound off, can you still read who wants what, what went wrong, and
   how they feel about it? If the story lives only in the dialogue — reject.
4. **The memorable-image test.** Is there one frame here a child would draw tomorrow? (The pollen
   moustache. The paws closed over empty gold. The upside-down legs in the blossom.) If every
   frame is forgettable — reject.
5. **The age-clarity test.** Will a four-year-old follow the surface without help — who, where,
   what happened — on one viewing? If the beat needs an adult to explain it — reject.
6. **The emotional-truth test.** Does anyone here behave the way a real person actually behaves
   under this feeling — hiding it, performing over it, leaking it sideways? If the emotion is
   worn on the outside of the acting instead of driving it — reject.
7. **The two-doors test.** Is there something for the child AND something for the adult in the
   same moment? One door open — reject or rework.
8. **The cost test.** Does the warmth at the end still carry a trace of what it cost? If the
   ending resets to zero — reject.

A beat must survive all eight. "It's fine" is a rejection; the standard is *someone will remember
this*.

## How this canon is used
This document is quoted into the Showrunner role's system prompt by `cb_creative.py`. It is
updated only via approved exemplars — a beat Julian has signed off may be added as a positive
example, and a beat he has rejected may be added as an anti-pattern, each with its reason. No
session rewrites the principles themselves.
