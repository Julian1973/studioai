# Cinematic prompt grammar

## Core principle

Write each prompt like a director and cinematographer handing a playable beat to an animation team. The language should create a readable sequence of cause, response, and change—not merely describe a beautiful frame.

## Shot sentence order

Use the following pattern flexibly:

`Shot N: size, focal length, camera behaviour. Subject + precise action. Spatial relationship and screen direction. Observable intention and reaction. Foreground/midground/background staging. Motivated light and material response. Exact line or editorial transition.`

Example anatomy:

`Shot 1: Medium shot, 50mm, tracking laterally. Zenny glides right-to-left in clean, measured arcs while Fuzzby scribbles through the midground behind her. Warm petal-filtered light catches the translucent edges of their wings. The contrast in their flight paths establishes their relationship before either speaks.`

## Directing choices

### Camera and lens

- 24–28mm: spatial, energetic, foreground exaggeration, environmental comedy
- 35mm: character within a readable world
- 50mm: natural relationship and two-shot coverage
- 65mm: performance-led medium close-up
- 85mm: intimate reaction, compressed background, facial detail

Use a focal length only when it helps produce a distinct visual result. Pair it with one clear behaviour: static, lateral track, push-in, pull-back, orbit, crane, handheld drift, whip-pan, or character-led follow.

### Physical action

Create visible causality:

`clips stem → pollen erupts → body yaws sideways → leaf rebounds → character recovers`

Avoid summary verbs such as “has an accident” or “flies chaotically” when the action can be staged beat by beat.

### Performance

Translate emotion into behaviour:

- confidence: chest first, fast recovery, refuses to acknowledge the mistake;
- suspicion: still body, narrowed eyes, delayed response;
- reluctant amusement: tries to suppress the smile, then one corner breaks;
- fear: breath catches, weight shifts back, eyes find the exit before the body moves.

Use intention, effort, interruption, recovery, and micro-reaction. Contrast performers when the relationship drives the scene.

### Composition

Specify only useful spatial information:

- screen direction and eyeline;
- foreground obstacle or frame;
- midground relationship;
- background landmark or leading line;
- depth change that supports action;
- negative space reserved for an arrival or reveal.

### Light, atmosphere, and materials

Use two to four story-relevant production details per shot:

- source: golden morning sun, cool skylight, lantern glow, reflected water;
- separation: rim light, pollen halo, mist shaft, edge light;
- bounce: yellow petal warming a face, blue crystal filling a shadow;
- material response: translucent wings, fuzzy fur breakup, wet stone sheen, drifting loose powder;
- depth: contact shadow, soft haze, foreground bokeh, ambient occlusion in cavities.

Do not decorate every shot with the same “cinematic” vocabulary. Light must belong to the established world and time of day.

### Editing

Use `Cut to.` only for an intended edit. Make each cut do something: reveal the consequence, land the joke, change point of view, or catch a reaction.

For a continuous shot, describe how the camera redirects or reframes instead. End on a usable image. A brief living hold may retain breathing, wing flutter, drifting dust, or other established ambient motion.

## Dialogue form

Place dialogue where it occurs:

`FUZZBY: Nailed it.`

Do not move all dialogue to the start or end. Preserve punctuation and words from the approved source. Describe vocal performance only when supported by the script, audio, or director.

## Density target

- One-shot clip: often 80–140 words
- Two-shot clip: often 150–230 words
- Three-shot clip: often 220–330 words

These are diagnostic ranges, not limits. Remove any sentence that does not change action, performance, composition, light, material response, sound, or edit.

## Avoid

- generic praise such as “epic”, “stunning”, or “Pixar-quality” without filmable detail;
- six simultaneous actions;
- contradictory camera instructions;
- prose that describes internal thoughts without observable behaviour;
- repeated identity descriptions already handled by references;
- a universal negative tail;
- arbitrary slow motion, drone moves, whip-pans, or lens changes;
- extra jokes, dialogue, characters, props, or story outcomes.
