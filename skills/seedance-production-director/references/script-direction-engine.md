# Script direction engine

Contents: purpose and scope; 1. script intake; 2. beat map; 3. screen idea; 4. editorial coverage; 5. paired prompts; 6. scene review; 7. delivery; evidence.

## Purpose and scope

Turn a script into specific screen storytelling and matching production prompts. Apply the creative compass in SKILL.md through decisions, alternatives, staging and review. These are an original working synthesis for Julian's production, not a reconstruction of anyone's private thought process or a promise of awards.

## 1. Read for the experience before dividing into clips

Read the whole supplied script once for the audience experience, then the relevant canon and scene details. Identify the protagonist's want, obstacle, emotional risk, relationship change, climax choice and ending. Name the audience question that carries the sequence. Mark setups and their payoffs across scenes; do not treat each clip as a new beginning.

Separate explicit facts from directorial interpretations and proposed script changes. Keep approved words, events, outcomes and character identities locked. When a scene lacks a causal turn or its stated feeling contradicts the action, explain the precise issue and propose the smallest script repair separately. Do not pretend camera polish solves absent drama. A request to direct allows performance and coverage invention; it does not silently approve a rewrite.

Use the supplied age range and tone. Do not impose preschool pacing on every family IP. Do not import an example character, prop, gag or location from this skill into canon.

## 2. Build the dramatic beat map

Give scenes, beats, shots and generation units separate stable IDs, such as SC02, SC02-B03, SC02-S05 and SC02-G02. A beat is a change; a shot is an uninterrupted camera view; a generation unit is a provider job which may contain several shots. A 30-second unit is not automatically one shot or a whole scene.

For each meaningful beat record:

| Field | Decision required |
|---|---|
| Source | Exact script passage or line IDs |
| Viewpoint | Whose experience guides audience attention |
| Want and tactic | What the character tries to achieve now, and how |
| Pressure | What makes that attempt difficult or revealing |
| Emotional change | Before, trigger, after; describe the visible evidence |
| Audience knowledge | What is known, withheld, discovered or reinterpreted |
| Comedy | Specific mechanism and payoff, or not applicable |
| Consequence | What changes and causes the next beat |
| Must-see moment | The expression, action or relationship the camera must catch |

Work from intention to behaviour: impulse, attempt, interruption, adjustment and consequence. Choose the relevant phases rather than making every performance a five-part routine. Give listeners their own attitude and attention; avoid a room of idle characters awaiting lines.

## 3. Develop a governing screen idea

For pivotal comic or emotional beats, consider two or three genuinely different staging approaches before selecting one. Examples of different approaches are a held relationship two-shot, an eyeline-led reveal, or a prop action that changes meaning. Lens substitutions alone are not alternatives.

Choose using emotional specificity, surprise, causal readability, originality within canon, audio feasibility and continuity cost. Deliver the selected idea and a brief reason; include alternatives only if useful to the user. Do not expose a long internal debate.

Find one physical expression of the scene's tension: distance between friends, a repeated attempt, an obstructed view, an offered space or an existing object handled differently. Let its meaning develop with the relationship. Avoid adding props or plot events without approval.

For comedy, decide what expectation the audience forms and when it is overturned. Let a character's sincere tactic create the consequence. A callback must change or deepen an earlier setup. For vulnerability, locate the moment the character risks being seen and the response that changes the relationship. Stage joy, discomfort and relief as specific bodily choices. Combine humour and emotion when the same action naturally carries both; do not require both in every beat.

## 4. Design editorial coverage before prompts

Map the room or location from approved sources: character positions, landmarks, movement paths and interaction axes. Derive camera positions from this map. Preserve world positions across reverses; screen coordinates change with camera position. Do not mirror the set to obtain a reverse angle. In ensembles, establish a new interaction axis when attention shifts between pairs.

For every shot specify:

- Beat ID and the information or feeling the shot contributes.
- Camera position relative to characters and landmarks, height, size, angle, lens feel, axis side and focal subject.
- Blocking, screen position, depth, eyeline target, visible face/body tell and important silhouette.
- Static camera or a motivated move with a start, trigger and endpoint.
- Cut-in action phase, cut-out trigger and the next shot's relationship to it.
- Speaker and listener, approved audio interval when available, and whether speech continues off screen.
- Opening state, action progression, closing state and generation-unit assignment.

Choose coverage deliberately from the following vocabulary. There is no quota of angles or cuts.

| Technique | Use when | Define precisely |
|---|---|---|
| Held wide or two-shot | Body language, distance or simultaneous action carries the beat | Readable silhouettes and where attention changes within the frame |
| Over-shoulder and reverse | The other person's response changes meaning | Foreground shoulder, focal character, matching eyelines, axis and reveal timing |
| Look / point of view / response | Discovery depends on what someone sees | Gaze target, what is withheld, and the reaction after discovery |
| Close reaction over a speaker's line | Listening is more revealing than speaking | Correct off-screen speaker and silent listener; preserve audio timing |
| High or low angle | Relative scale, access to space or subjective experience benefits | Concrete viewpoint; avoid automatic high-equals-weak symbolism |
| Insert | A small action or object state supplies essential information | Exact prop/hand state and its causal connection to adjacent shots |
| Match on action | Movement bridges a cut | Matching action phase, direction and speed without repeating the action |
| Reveal through blocking or camera | New information should arrive during one shot | What initially occludes it and the motivated reveal trigger |
| Depth staging or focus shift | Attention transfers between related subjects | Initial and final focal targets; keep geography legible |
| Contrast of wide and intimate | Scale and private feeling strengthen each other | Why the change of distance occurs at this beat |

A reverse is a new camera view, not reverse playback. A crop does not manufacture a reverse. A small punch-in is not automatically a meaningful new shot. Do not alternate speakers mechanically or put a camera move on every line.

Use straight cuts by default. Describe J/L cuts as approved sound continuing across the picture cut, with exact ownership; do not shift words or create new audio. Preserve speaking mouth sync whenever visible. Choose pauses from measured performances or label timing as an estimate during development; never prescribe automatic frame holds. Reserve time for anticipation and the emotional landing. If coverage exceeds the unit budget, split generation units while preserving the scene's rhythm.

## 5. Compile stills and animation from the same record

Use the shared shot record as the authority for both prompts. Never author them as independent interpretations of the screenplay. Use the installed Seedream image-director skill for image prompt syntax and canon checks when available; use this skill's audio and prompt grammar references for animation. If a companion skill is unavailable, retain this shared-state contract and state any missing provider-specific verification.

Produce only the assets the coverage needs:

- Scene plate: clean environment with no characters, approved geography, light and camera view. Create additional angle plates when the reverse exposes unverified geography; do not assume a single front view defines the back of the set.
- Keyframe: one exact instant at a shot or generation unit's opening, containing the required characters and action preparation. Give faces, limbs and props room for the planned action. Do not show a later reaction, expanded prop state or completed movement prematurely.
- Additional shot reference: a planned reverse or insert where needed. Label whether it is for planning, identity, composition or an actual provider input; do not invent host support for multiple shot-start images.
- Landing reference: only when a closing state needs visual control and the verified route supports its use. Distinguish target from generated result.

For each shot pair preserve identical shot/beat IDs, canon version, character counts and proportions, wardrobe, prop state, world positions, gaze, starting camera, light and time. The animation begins at that keyframe's exact state, then supplies action, performance, cuts and sound. Write still prompts as a single visible moment, not sequential actions. The first animation action must be physically possible from the still.

For a continuous join, inherit the approved final frame and motion phase. For an intentional cut to a reverse, inherit the world and action state but establish the new camera view; do not simultaneously require pixel-identical frames. Specify whether the cut is inside a native multishot unit or between separate units assembled in the edit. Verify the selected provider route before promising the operation.

Carry one uninterrupted approved audio timeline across visual coverage. If units require segments, record their source in/out offsets and whether they are prepared and accepted by the route; do not claim an audio file exists or alter its performance. Keep provider input bindings separate from internal asset IDs. Missing actual inputs remain null and block the affected production prompt, not the upstream plan.

## 6. Run the scene before polishing it

Read the proposed shots in editorial order as a paper story reel. Ask what the audience understands and feels at each cut. When approved boards, audio or clips exist and tools permit, inspect the actual reel; call a paper review a paper review, not a screening or render test.

Check these observable conditions:

1. Every script beat maps to a visible event or intentionally off-screen event with a visible consequence; no orphan shots or omitted payoffs.
2. The pivotal reaction is readable at the moment it occurs, with sufficient available time.
3. The comic expectation and consequence connect, and vulnerability is not turned into ridicule.
4. Reverses have matching eyelines, plausible backgrounds and consistent world positions.
5. Image opening states exactly match animation starts; the payoff is not baked into the opening frame.
6. Dialogue, movement, discovery and landing fit the actual audio and duration; estimates remain labelled until measured.
7. Muting dialogue still leaves the main physical action and emotional change legible; do not demand that every verbal fact be visually reconstructible.
8. Removing music does not destroy the causal story. Music may legitimately contribute emotional force when approved.
9. An equally attractive generic shot would lose something specific: the chosen framing earns its place.

Repair the first substantive failure and recheck affected dependencies. If a shot changes, prepare a new version of only its affected direction, image/audio briefs and animation prompt. Preserve every other approved artifact. Report continuity consequences for later shots as proposed changes; do not rewrite or invalidate them automatically. Treat an unchanged handoff as no downstream change. Stop when these checks pass or identify the specific unresolved story/input issue. A self-assigned score cannot certify audience impact or award quality. Actual generated performances and audience response remain evidence for later refinement.

## 7. Deliver the production package

Keep this order unless the user requests prompt-only output:

1. Concise episode/scene intent and the selected screen idea.
2. Scenes containing beats and editorial shots, with generation units mapped separately.
3. Needed scene plates and opening keyframes, with stable IDs and prompts where ready.
4. Matching animation prompts, audio mapping and editorial cut instructions where ready.
5. Opening/closing continuity, readiness per deliverable, and concrete unresolved inputs.
6. Review outcome and any separately proposed script revision.

Use the structured supplement in output-contract.md for integrations. Do not claim to update StudioAI code, generate media, operate CapCut or deploy a pipeline merely by saving this skill. Do not spend on media generation unless the user's request authorises it.

## Evidence behind the creative compass

Read these as contextual sources, not as a proprietary formula. The operational choices above are our synthesis. Do not attribute all shot techniques or review rules to the named filmmakers.

- Joe Brumm: [Peabody's creator interview page](https://peabodyawards.com/stories/bluey-creator-joe-brumm/) describes discussion of play and humour for children and parents. Only the page summary was reviewed; its linked transcript was unavailable on 7 September 2026.
- Pete Docter: [D23's direct interview](https://d23.com/interview-inside-out-brain-trust-pete-docter/) connects observing his daughter's change in behaviour with the premise of Inside Out and animation's expressive possibilities. [No Film School's interview overview](https://nofilmschool.com/the-art-of-iteration-with-pixars-pete-docter) describes iteration, vulnerability and relatable emotion; the audio was not reviewed.
- Chris Sanders: [The Credits' direct interview](https://www.motionpictures.org/2024/10/the-wild-robot-writer-director-chris-sanders-on-kindness-as-a-survival-skill/) discusses preserving the story's core, physical landscape expressing an emotional moment, personally boarding the migration, and planning dialogue-free musical passages. Apply any music-led approach only when the production's approved audio workflow permits it.
